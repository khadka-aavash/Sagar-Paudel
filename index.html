Profile

About me

Where ecological science, rural innovation and global policy meet practice.

Currently pursuing a Master's degree in Development and Rural Innovation at Wageningen University & Research, I specialise in knowledge, technology and innovation. My work connects scientific research, policy frameworks and local agricultural practice to drive sustainable, inclusive rural transformation and ecosystem restoration.

At Wetlands International I research the socio-economic barriers to scaling nature-based solutions as part of the NL2120 initiative in the Netherlands and globally. Alongside that research, my leadership with the International Association of Students in Agriculture and Related Sciences (IAAS) has taken me to global platforms — including COP27, COP28, SB62 and the World Food Forum — advocating for youth-led climate action and resilient food systems.

Ongoing

Research project

Socio-economic barriers to NbS (NL2120)

Identifying the socio-economic and institutional barriers to implementing nature-based solutions in the Netherlands and beyond, with Wetlands International.

  • Wetlands International
  • NL2120

Applied research with Wetlands International on why nature-based solutions that work ecologically still struggle to scale.

NL2120 asks what the Dutch landscape could look like if nature did more of the engineering — wetter peat, wider rivers, hedgerows doing the work of infrastructure. The ecological case is largely settled. This research looks at what is not.

What the work looks at

  • Land tenure, and who holds the right to change how land is used
  • Financing and insurance that cover the transition years, not only the outcome
  • Succession planning, and the gap between who carries the risk and who collects the benefit
  • Institutional and policy pathways that help or block landscape-scale rollout

02 Dec 2023

Keynote speech

Financing the food systems transition from the ground up

Adaptation money moves through capitals and conference halls. The farms it is meant for are three intermediaries away — and that distance is the design flaw.

  • Climate finance
  • Food systems

Delivered at the Food Systems Pavilion during the second week of COP28, to an audience of negotiators, funders and farmer organisations.

Every year we arrive at these halls with better data than the year before. We can map a peatland's carbon flux from orbit. We can model a rice yield under four degrees of warming. What we still cannot do, reliably, is get a thousand euros into the hands of a farmer who has already decided to change how she farms.

Adaptation finance does not have a volume problem so much as a last-mile problem. The money exists. The plumbing does not.

In the Netherlands, the NL2120 programme is asking what the landscape would look like if nature did the engineering — wetter peat, wider rivers, hedgerows doing the work of infrastructure. The ecology of that question is largely settled. What stalls the work is tenure, insurance, succession planning, and the fact that the farmer carrying the transition risk is rarely the party collecting the benefit.

So I want to propose three tests for any financing instrument announced this week. First: can a farm of ten hectares access it without hiring a consultant? Second: does it pay for the transition years, not just the outcome? Third: who holds the risk if the first harvest fails — and have we asked them?

12 Jun 2025

Media address

Adaptation has a last-mile problem

Remarks to press at the Bonn climate conference on why adaptation pledges keep arriving without the institutions to spend them well.

  • Adaptation
  • UNFCCC

Prepared remarks for a side-event press briefing during the June sessions in Bonn.

Three things are usually true at once at these meetings. The pledge is real. The gap is real. And nobody in the room can say, with a straight face, which institution will move the money the final fifty kilometres to a district cooperative.

That last mile is not a logistics footnote. It is where adaptation either becomes a practice or stays a line item — and it is staffed, almost everywhere, by extension services that have been defunded for two decades.
